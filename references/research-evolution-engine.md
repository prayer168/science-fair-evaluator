# Research Evolution Engine（研究演化引擎）

## Purpose

Use this engine when an idea is common, repetitive, descriptive, unmeasurable, overly difficult, or dependent on impressive technology without a clear scientific contribution.

The goal is not to make the topic sound newer. The goal is to create a stronger evidence chain.

## Evolution Record

Always summarize the redesign with:

`原始題材 → 問題診斷 → 升級策略 → 新研究缺口 → 最終題目`

## Step 1 — Diagnose the Weakness

Check whether the seed is mainly:

- a known demonstration;
- a “which material/brand is best” comparison;
- a broad environmental or health claim;
- a literature-only topic;
- a survey without mechanism;
- a black-box sensor or AI application;
- a project with no control group;
- a project with subjective outcomes;
- a project requiring adult operation for the meaningful work;
- a topic already repeated extensively in science fairs.

## Step 2 — Select Evolution Paths

### A. Mechanism Evolution

Move from “what happens” to “why and under what conditions it happens.”

Examples:

- material comparison → physical or chemical property responsible for the difference;
- plant growth comparison → light, moisture, temperature, or nutrient mechanism;
- filtration efficiency → particle size, flow resistance, adsorption, or layer structure.

### B. Measurement Evolution

Replace subjective judgment with measurable indicators.

Possible upgrades:

- image analysis;
- mass, time, distance, angle, temperature, conductivity, pH, turbidity, sound level, light intensity;
- standardized scoring rubric with inter-rater checks;
- calibration curves;
- normalized values by area, mass, time, or initial condition.

### C. Experimental-Control Evolution

Upgrade one-shot comparisons through:

- control groups;
- repeated trials;
- randomized order;
- fixed starting conditions;
- pilot testing;
- calibration and blank tests;
- sensitivity or reliability checks.

### D. Context Evolution

Apply known science to a meaningful local condition:

- schoolyard or classroom;
- local climate, water, soil, plants, waste, energy use, or community practice;
- seasonal or field validation;
- comparison between laboratory and real-world conditions.

Local context is not automatically novel. It must create a testable question or useful validation.

### E. Prototype Evolution

Turn evidence into design iteration:

1. measure baseline performance;
2. identify a mechanism or failure;
3. build prototype 1;
4. test using fixed criteria;
5. redesign based on evidence;
6. compare prototype 1 and prototype 2;
7. validate under realistic conditions.

### F. Modeling Evolution

Build a simple prediction from observed variables, then test it with new data.

Suitable models include:

- linear or curved trend;
- ratio or normalized index;
- threshold;
- classification rule;
- empirical score;
- simple physical model students can explain.

Do not use machine learning when a transparent formula is sufficient.

### G. Technology Evolution

Use Arduino, ESP32, sensors, computer vision, Python, AI, or IoT only if the technology:

- increases measurement frequency or precision;
- reduces human bias;
- enables long-term monitoring;
- detects a pattern that manual observation would miss;
- supports reproducible data collection;
- remains explainable and operable by students.

Technology is not the research question. Always identify the scientific variable and evidence contribution.

### H. Validation Evolution

Move beyond laboratory success through:

- blind or unseen test samples;
- a second location or time period;
- natural versus simulated conditions;
- prototype field test;
- comparison against a reference instrument or established method;
- replication by another student group.

## Step 3 — Form the Research Gap

Write a defensible gap using one of these patterns:

- Existing work tested X, but did not control Y.
- Prior projects compared materials, but did not measure the mechanism.
- Laboratory demonstrations exist, but local field performance remains unclear.
- Existing methods are accurate but too costly; a low-cost measurement method needs validation.
- A prototype has been demonstrated, but its reliability, threshold, or long-term stability is unknown.
- Prior work reports average performance, but the effect of position, timing, scale, or environmental variation remains uncertain.

Avoid absolute novelty claims.

## Step 4 — Evaluate the Evolved Topic

The evolved topic must pass:

- safety and ethics;
- clear manipulated variable;
- measurable dependent variable;
- realistic controls and repeats;
- elementary student ownership;
- available time and materials;
- defensible contribution;
- understandable analysis.

## Step 5 — Produce the Final Topic Package

Output:

1. original topic diagnosis;
2. evolution path used;
3. evidence-backed research gap;
4. final recommended title;
5. core research question;
6. novelty type;
7. minimum viable experiment;
8. advanced extension;
9. failure fallback;
10. reason the evolved topic is stronger.
